## [Ancient DNA shows widespread selection in West Eurasia](https://www.nature.com/articles/s41586-026-10358-1) ⭐️ 9.0/10

A Nature study published on 15 April 2026 analyzed 15,836 ancient West Eurasian genomes and reported hundreds of cases of directional selection. Using an ancient-DNA time-series method that tests for consistent allele-frequency shifts over time, the authors argue that sustained adaptive change was widespread rather than rare. This changes a common view in recent human evolutionary genetics that strong, sustained directional selection was relatively uncommon over the past several millennia in West Eurasia. Because the dataset is exceptionally large and directly tracks allele-frequency change through time, the study could reshape how researchers study adaptation in traits such as immunity, pigmentation, and behavior. The paper emphasizes that ancient DNA can reveal true directional selection only if analyses can separate sustained fitness-driven allele increases from changes caused by migration, population structure, drift, or other non-adaptive processes. The authors applied their method to 15,836 West Eurasians, including 10,016 newly reported individuals, which substantially increased statistical power compared with earlier ancient-DNA selection scans.

rss · Nature · Apr 15, 00:00

**Background**: Ancient DNA research studies genetic material recovered from archaeological human remains, allowing scientists to observe how allele frequencies changed across real historical time instead of inferring the past only from present-day genomes. Directional selection refers to a process in which one genetic variant is consistently favored, causing its frequency to rise over generations. In human ancient-DNA studies, detecting such selection is difficult because migrations and population mixing can mimic adaptive allele-frequency shifts. Earlier work had already shown the promise of time-stratified ancient genomes for studying adaptation, but limited sample size and confounding from ancestry made broad conclusions harder to establish.

## [MitoCatch enables targeted mitochondrial transplants](https://www.nature.com/articles/s41586-026-10391-0) ⭐️ 9.0/10

A Nature paper published online on 15 April 2026 reports MitoCatch, a cell-type-specific mitochondrial transplantation system that uses protein binders to link donor mitochondria to target cell surfaces and deliver them into those cells. The study says this targeted delivery rescued cell degeneration, extending mitochondrial transplantation beyond untargeted uptake approaches. Mitochondrial dysfunction contributes to degeneration and disease in many tissues, so a method that can selectively deliver healthy mitochondria to the right cells could make mitochondrial therapy more practical and effective. This is especially important because specificity may improve uptake and reduce waste compared with broadly exposing mixed cell populations to donor mitochondria. According to the paper summary and related coverage, MitoCatch works by binder-mediated attachment between mitochondria and the target cell surface, then promotes mitochondrial internalization into selected cell types. Coverage cited examples including neurons, retinal, cardiac, endothelial and immune cells, and noted higher uptake than untargeted delivery, though the available summary does not provide full clinical or long-term safety data.

rss · Nature · Apr 15, 00:00

**Background**: Mitochondria are the cell’s energy-producing organelles, and mitochondrial dysfunction has been linked to a wide range of degenerative and metabolic disorders. Mitochondrial transplantation is an emerging therapeutic idea in which healthy mitochondria are transferred into damaged cells to restore function, but previous approaches have generally struggled with efficient and selective delivery. MitoCatch fits into this broader effort by adding cell-type targeting, which could help move mitochondrial therapy toward more controlled biomedical applications.

## [Distilled LLMs can inherit hidden behavioral traits](https://www.nature.com/articles/s41586-026-10319-8) ⭐️ 9.0/10

A Nature paper published on 15 April 2026 reports that large language models can transmit latent behavioral traits during model distillation through hidden signals in generated training data. The study shows that a student model can inherit traits from a teacher model even when those traits are not explicitly present in the apparent content of the data. This matters because distillation and synthetic data are widely used to make model training cheaper and faster, including in alignment and safety pipelines. If undesirable behaviors can survive filtering and transfer through semantically unrelated data, then current evaluation, reuse, and alignment practices may be much less reliable than assumed. The related Anthropic write-up and arXiv preprint describe this phenomenon as “subliminal learning,” where a teacher with a trait can induce that trait in a student via data such as number sequences that do not explicitly mention it. The reported setup highlights a serious caveat for distill-and-filter strategies: removing obvious problematic content from synthetic datasets may not be sufficient to prevent transmission of model behaviors.

rss · Nature · Apr 15, 00:00

**Background**: Model distillation is the practice of training a smaller or newer model on outputs produced by another “teacher” model, often to reduce cost or transfer capabilities. In modern AI development, synthetic data from language models is also used to shape alignment, improve instruction following, or filter unsafe content. AI alignment refers to methods for making model behavior match intended goals and human preferences. This new result is notable because it suggests that behavior can be encoded in training data in ways that are not obvious from the data’s surface meaning.

The process combines nanoimprinting with TiO2 coating to produce high-efficiency metalenses with uniform performance at industrial scale. Visible metalenses have long been promising because they can replace bulky curved optics with flat metasurfaces, but manufacturing at low cost and high volume has been a major bottleneck. Demonstrating 300-unit-per-second production suggests metasurface optics may be moving from laboratory prototypes toward practical commercialization in imaging, sensing, and compact optical systems. The reported method relies on roll-to-roll nanoimprint lithography, a known high-throughput replication approach, and applies TiO2 to realize efficient visible-light operation. The significance is not just the headline throughput, but the combination of low cost, high efficiency, and uniformity, which are all essential for commercial optical components.

rss · Nature · Apr 15, 00:00

**Background**: Metalenses are flat lenses built from metasurfaces, which use arrays of subwavelength nanostructures to shape the phase, amplitude, or polarization of light. In the visible range, these devices are especially challenging because they require features smaller than 100 nanometers and materials with low optical loss. Reviews of the field have highlighted that while metasurfaces offer thin, lightweight optical components, fabrication complexity has limited mass production. Roll-to-roll nanoimprint lithography is attractive in this context because it is a scalable patterning method designed for high-throughput replication of micro- and nanoscale features.

Applied to melanoma cells, it identified shared regulatory nodes and convergent dedifferentiated cell states associated with resistance to the BRAF inhibitor vemurafenib. Drug resistance in melanoma often arises through many different genetic changes, making it hard to find intervention points that generalize across tumors. PerturbFate matters because it aims to reveal common cell-state programs downstream of diverse perturbations, which could help functional genomics researchers and cancer biologists identify more robust therapeutic targets. The work extends the idea of pooled single-cell CRISPR perturbation screens by coupling CRISPR interference with multimodal readouts, specifically transcriptional and chromatin-accessibility changes, rather than relying on a single phenotype. The Nature summary highlights a key biological finding: distinct resistance-linked perturbations in melanoma push cells toward a shared dedifferentiated state, consistent with known melanoma plasticity under RAF inhibition.

CRISPRi is a gene-repression approach that uses a catalytically inactive Cas protein to reduce expression of targeted genes without cutting DNA, making it useful for systematic perturbation studies. In melanoma, resistance to vemurafenib, a BRAF inhibitor, has long been linked not only to specific mutations but also to reversible cell-state changes, including dedifferentiation, which makes convergent-state mapping especially relevant.

This is important because it moves beyond studying human or viral genetics in isolation and instead shows that cancer risk can depend on a specific combination of host and pathogen genotypes. That insight could improve biological understanding of EBV-associated cancers and support more precise risk stratification in populations where nasopharyngeal carcinoma is common. The paper uses a genome-to-genome framework, an approach that tests associations between host genetic variants and pathogen genetic variants to identify interaction points relevant to disease. A key technical focus is HLA, a highly polymorphic immune gene region involved in antigen presentation, which makes it biologically plausible that host immune genotype could shape the effects of different EBV strains.

rss · Nature · Apr 15, 00:00

Epstein–Barr virus is a common human virus that has long been linked to several cancers, including nasopharyngeal carcinoma. Prior work has also suggested that EBV strain variation may contribute to the striking geographic distribution of EBV-associated cancers, making host–virus joint analysis a logical next step.

## [AI makes cybersecurity a compute race](https://www.dbreunig.com/2026/04/14/cybersecurity-is-proof-of-work-now.html) ⭐️ 8.0/10

This essay argues that LLMs are changing cybersecurity economics by turning vulnerability discovery and software hardening into a scalable, compute-intensive contest. The core claim is that both defenders and attackers can use AI to search more code, test more hypotheses, and automate more security work, making security resemble a proof-of-work race. If this framing is correct, software security may depend less on purely manual review and more on who can apply the most effective models, context, and compute to a codebase or attack surface. That would affect vendors, security teams, and attackers alike, while reinforcing a broader industry trend toward AI-assisted software engineering and automated security analysis. The discussion highlights an important caveat: current LLM-based vulnerability scanning can be inefficient, especially when it naively scans files one by one without richer program analysis. Grounding from recent survey literature also suggests that techniques such as AST analysis, data-flow or control-flow analysis, retrieval, and program slicing help LLMs use context better, but cross-file and complex vulnerabilities remain difficult.

hackernews · dbreunig · Apr 14, 18:08

**Background**: In security, vulnerability discovery means finding flaws in software that attackers could exploit, while hardening means reducing attack surface through safer code, configuration, and validation. LLM-based security tools try to assist this process by reading code, generating hypotheses about weaknesses, and sometimes combining language models with traditional static-analysis signals. The article's “proof of work” metaphor does not refer to blockchain consensus directly, but to the idea that security outcomes may increasingly depend on expending large amounts of computation and analysis effort. Recent research surveys indicate that LLMs can help with vulnerability detection, especially when paired with structured code context, but they still struggle with deeper program reasoning and complex multi-file bugs.
