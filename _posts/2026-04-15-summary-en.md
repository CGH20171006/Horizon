A Technology Review feature examines a synthetic biology idea discussed at a 2019 NSF-linked high-risk workshop: building “mirror” bacteria made from biomolecules with opposite chirality to known life. The article highlights that the concept remains speculative, but scientists and ethicists are increasingly focused on whether such organisms could create unprecedented ecological and biosecurity dangers. Mirror-life microbes matter because they could interact very differently with immune systems, predators, and decomposers that evolved for ordinary biology, making containment and response unusually hard. The story also reflects a broader governance challenge in frontier biotech: deciding whether some technically fascinating experiments should be slowed or avoided before the capability fully exists. The central technical idea is chirality: life on Earth relies on biomolecules with specific handedness, and a mirror organism would be built from reversed versions of those components. Sources cited in the broader discussion note that mirror bacteria do not currently exist, and some researchers argue they should not be created because natural biological defenses and ecological checks might not work as expected against them.

rss · MIT Technology Review · Apr 15, 09:00

**Background**: In biology, many key molecules are chiral, meaning they exist in left- and right-handed forms that are not interchangeable. Known life uses one consistent set of chiral building blocks for proteins and nucleic acids, which is why a “mirror” organism would be fundamentally different from ordinary cells. Mirror-bacteria research is often discussed within xenobiology, a field that explores alternative biological systems beyond those found in nature. Recent scientific commentary has emphasized that while mirror biomolecules may have useful applications such as more durable drugs, extending that work to whole mirror organisms raises much larger safety questions.
